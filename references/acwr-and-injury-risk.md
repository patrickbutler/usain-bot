# Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio — Use and Limits

The acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR), popularized by Tim Gabbett's
work in team sports, compares a short-term training load window
(commonly 7 days) to a longer rolling average (commonly 28 days) as a
proxy for how much load is "spiking" relative to what the athlete is
adapted to. Ratios in the roughly 0.8-1.3 range are conventionally
described as a "sweet spot," with higher ratios associated with
elevated injury risk in several observational cohorts.

Methodologically, ACWR has been criticized on several grounds: the
choice of 7-day/28-day windows is somewhat arbitrary, the ratio can be
mathematically unstable at low chronic-load values, rolling-average
versus exponentially-weighted moving average formulations produce
different numbers from the same underlying data, and the observational
studies underpinning the "sweet spot" don't establish causation. Several
more recent papers argue ACWR should be treated as one directional
signal among several, not a sole gate for training decisions.

Practical takeaway for a returning runner with a joint injury history:
treat a rising or elevated ACWR as a reason to hold or reduce load, but
don't treat a "green zone" ACWR alone as license to push volume or
intensity — corroborate it with the athlete's actual completed
long-run capacity, adherence pattern, and any subjective flags before
authorizing an increase.
